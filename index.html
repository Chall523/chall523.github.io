01 / Wearable medical device

SMART-Cuff: monitoring shoulder recovery outside the clinic

The problem. Patients recovering from rotator cuff repair need to regain movement without overexerting the healing shoulder. Traditional support devices have limited ability to characterize actual use or provide individualized feedback.

The engineering approach. Our senior design team is developing a sleeve-based sensing concept that combines arm and torso motion sensing, potential muscle activity measurement, and feedback. The design explores compensatory movement detection, secure sensor attachment, comfort, and phase-specific thresholds.

My technical focus. Early system architecture, sensor integration and testing, including evaluation of wearable temperature measurements and changes in placement. The current work focuses on measurement reliability before making clinical claims.

Next steps. Develop repeatable calibration, assess signal quality across daily wear conditions, establish movement-related ground truth, and test feedback with appropriate expert input.

Evidence to show

01 · Actual brace with IMU and thermistor placement annotated

02 · System block diagram and wiring architecture

03 · Stationary versus movement sensor traces

04 · Short test clip showing motion detection

05 · Design iterations and calibration limitations

Status: university team prototype, not a cleared or clinically validated product.

02 / Robotics & mechanical design

A gripper designed for objects that deform under contact

The problem. Conventional hard robotic jaws can slip on or deform fragile, spherical, water-filled objects. The challenge is balancing secure grasping, predictable alignment, speed, and minimal squeeze force.

The design approach. I am exploring a compact parallel-jaw mechanism with 3D-printed fingers, textured silicone interfaces, and force-sensitive resistors beneath the contact area. A close-range camera may help locate the object and estimate deformation, while a servo controls jaw closure.

Key constraints. Restricted arm travel, camera working distance, geometry of the fixed pick-and-place setup, limited contact area, and the need to avoid confusing successful positioning with a successful grasp.

Next steps. Characterize servo motion, fabricate and compare finger geometries, calibrate force signals, and measure grasp success and object deformation over repeated trials.

Evidence to show

01 · Multi-view CAD renders and fingertip sections

02 · Force-sensitive resistor circuit and calibration

03 · Servo control code and physical prototype photo

04 · Camera field-of-view and working-distance study

05 · Pick-and-place trial video, when tested

Status: design and early implementation; success rates are not yet established.

03 / Computer vision

Peekabot: recovering a visual target after it disappears

The problem. A moving camera or robot may lose its target when detection fails, the object leaves the field of view, or an occlusion occurs. A fresh bounding box alone does not prove that the original target has been recovered.

The approach. A modular perception system uses object detection and time-aware observations, with planned motion prediction, identity consistency checks, and explicit recovery guidance. The implementation distinguishes observed, predicted, and unconfirmed states.

Engineering discipline. Component contracts and automated tests help separate what is already implemented from broader ambitions such as physical robot control or full scene understanding.

Next steps. Benchmark the detector on actual footage, quantify target-loss and recovery scenarios, and validate full-stack recovery behavior using live experiments rather than only offline replay.

Evidence to show

01 · Side-by-side target observation frames

02 · Tracking and state-machine architecture

03 · Detector/test results and latency benchmark

04 · One challenging occlusion or exit/reentry sequence

05 · Documented failure modes and what changed

Status: active student project; repository is currently private.
